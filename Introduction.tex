\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a dominant
paradigm for improving the reasoning abilities of large language models
(LLMs)~\citep{cite}. To improve throughput, current RL post-training frameworks
decouple rollout generation from gradient computation, delegating them to an
inference engine and a training engine respectively~\citep{cite}. Although the
two engines load the same parameters, differences in their numerical
implementations lead them to assign different probabilities to the same token,
so that nominally on-policy training is in fact off-policy~\citep{cite}. This
\emph{training--inference mismatch} is particularly severe on mixture-of-experts
models, and can cause training instability or even collapse~\citep{cite}.

The standard remedy for this mismatch is importance sampling, which reweights
the gradient of each token by the ratio of the two engines'
probabilities~\citep{cite}. However, the two engines occasionally disagree
sharply on individual tokens, making this ratio extremely large, so that the
exact correction yields gradient estimates with uncontrolled variance. The key
question is therefore not whether to correct, but how to truncate. Moreover, a
deviation of the same size has different significance at different confidence
levels: on a highly confident token, the typical mismatch is much narrower, so
the same deviation is more anomalous; on a less-confident token, larger
deviations arise naturally from the broader mismatch distribution.

Existing methods largely rely on heuristic rules. At the token level, TIS caps
the ratio at a fixed upper bound~\citep{cite}, IcePop masks tokens whose ratio
falls outside a fixed interval~\citep{cite}, and KPop masks by a KL
threshold~\citep{cite}; at the sequence level, existing methods truncate or
reject the ratio of the whole response~\citep{cite}, or replace the token-level
objective with a sequence-level one~\citep{cite}; other work approaches the
problem from numerical precision and switches the entire pipeline to
FP16~\citep{cite}. Although these methods are effective to varying degrees,
their thresholds are all independent of token confidence, and none of them
characterizes \emph{how much to truncate} from the statistical structure of the
mismatch itself. In practice, fixed thresholds take effect predominantly on
the least confident tokens, while substantial mismatches on high-confidence
tokens can remain untreated. This gap motivates the central question:

\begin{center}
\emph{Can we design a principled mismatch correction grounded in the \\
statistical structure of the training--inference mismatch itself?}
\end{center}

We answer this question affirmatively by proposing \textbf{CIS}---\underline{\textbf{C}}alibrated
\underline{\textbf{I}}mportance \underline{\textbf{S}}ampling---as illustrated
in \Cref{fig:cis_pipeline}. CIS imposes on the importance weight of each token
an upper bound that varies with its confidence, suppressing numerical noise
while preserving the learning signal. At its core is a truncation criterion
derived from the statistical structure of the mismatch: confidence calibration
determines the shape of the truncation band, while bias--variance minimization
determines its slope. Our contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item We propose a logit-displacement model of the training--inference
mismatch: the mismatch manifests as an additive displacement $\varepsilon_t$
in the log-odds of the sampled token, with a confidence-invariant bulk
distribution and a sparse residual component that may depend on token
confidence. We further trace this displacement back to the per-logit
perturbation before the softmax: the bulk is consistent with floating-point
rounding, while routing disagreement is an important contributor to the heavy
tail in mixture-of-experts models.

\item Under this model, we formulate mismatch correction as a bias--variance
minimization problem in which importance weights may only be attenuated, never
amplified. We show that calibrating the truncation to clip the same fraction of
bulk tokens across confidence levels yields a band whose width grows linearly
with token uncertainty, reducing the remaining optimization to a single slope
parameter. Theoretically, we show that CIS replaces the potentially explosive
second-moment dependence $\E[e^{2\varepsilon_t}]$ of exact importance sampling
with a uniformly bounded truncated second moment, while its induced bias is
controlled by the first moment $\E[e^{\varepsilon_t}]$.

\item Empirically, we show that CIS outperforms existing token-level and
sequence-level baselines across multiple mathematical reasoning benchmarks.
Diagnostic analyses further support our theoretical claims and validate the
necessity of confidence-aware truncation.
\end{itemize}
